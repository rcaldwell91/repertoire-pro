# Repertoire — Train update plan (3 Nov)

This is a plan only: nothing in the app has changed. It compares Robert's approved spec (3 Nov) with Train as it is on main. Main was read on 2 Nov; its latest change is from 29 Oct (commit b864a06), and nothing newer exists. Robert's rule for Train (3 Nov) is applied throughout: Train only includes what a singer can learn by reading and using the app's tools.

Words in quotes are the app's or the spec's own. Everything else is a proposal, and the decisions that are Robert's to make are listed at the end. This plan contains nothing from any course.

## 1. Five things that affect every item

### 1.1 The page layout today, and where each part goes

An exercise page today has, in this order: the title (the proper name), the plain name as a small line under it (only where the title is jargon, e.g. "Straw singing"), the pattern card, "Goal" (one line), "How" (one sentence), and Start. Behind one tap ("More about this") are "Why", "Watch for" and "Care". The level, Quiet and what is measured are shown on the list row, not on the page.

How today's parts map to the spec's layout:
- **Title:** unchanged.
- **Plain name** (e.g. "Straw singing"): the spec doesn't mention it. Proposal: keep it as the small line under the title, as decided on 27–28 Oct.
- **Goal** becomes the plain one-line description.
- **How** becomes one of the numbered steps in "How to do it".
- **Why** becomes "Why it helps", and **Watch for** becomes "Watch out for".
- **Care** has no section in the spec. Proposal: Care's points become the last points of "Watch out for", so nothing is lost. This matters for Messa di voce, where the warning about not going to full volume lives in Care today.
- **"You're doing it right when"** has no match today. It is new for every exercise.
- **"You'll need"** exists today only on Straw ("You'll need a straw.").

**Which parts are on the page and which are behind one tap.** The rulebook's tier rule (24 Oct) puts the first tier on the page and the second behind one tap. Six sections plus numbered steps would push Start down a phone screen.
- **Proposal:** on the page go the title, plain line, You'll need, How to do it and Start. Behind "More about this" go "You're doing it right when", "Watch out for" and "Why it helps", in that order.
- **Alternative:** put "You're doing it right when" on the page as well.
- This is Robert's call.

### 1.2 The wording rules the app enforces today clash with the spec

The app's wording checks hold every line to 8 words. The How line may have up to 14 words, in one sentence. Robert's rule of 25 Oct says How is "never which sound, never how many notes", because the pattern card already shows both. A list of singer jargon is also banned from the plain name, Goal and How. The old explanations behind "More about this" are checked only for banned words, not for length.

The spec breaks these rules, as written:
- **Line length.** Posture check step 1 is 20 words. Tongue-out's new cloth line is 31 words.
- **Naming the sound.** Bratty Nay's first step names its sound ("nay, nay, nay"). The tension check's step 2 names "ah".
- **Counting notes.** Steps count notes, e.g. "three notes up and back" and "the five-note scale".

**Proposal:** the new step lists, "doing it right", "watch out" and "why" are checked the way the old explanations are, for banned words with no length cap. The jargon list still applies to the plain line, You'll need and the steps; none of the spec's step lines uses a word on it. "Why it helps" is checked like the old "Why". That matters because items 3 and 9 say "vocal folds", which is on the jargon list for the plain line, Goal and How. The old "Why" texts already use words from that list, such as "larynx". The 3 Nov spec replaces the 25 Oct How rule for steps, since the later decision wins. Robert to confirm.

The on-screen lines in part C and item 7 fit the 8-word cap, with one exception: "Turn your head slowly, or let it roll loosely." is 9 words. **Options:** allow 9 for these lines, or shorten it to "Turn your head slowly, or let it roll." (8 words).

### 1.3 Levels inside one exercise are new

Today each exercise has one level and one pattern. The only exercise with a choice is Sing the interval: "Which shapes" in the gear's sound sheet, remembered on the phone. The spec needs levels inside three exercises: La-Ga (three levels), Bratty Nay (two) and Straw singing (three).

**Proposal:**
- Use the same picker for all three: a level row in the gear's sound sheet, remembered for each exercise. The pattern card and the notes follow the chosen level.
- The list row shows the span ("Beginner to Advanced") instead of one level.
- **Routines:** a routine uses its own level (Daily, Quiet and Cool-down = Beginner; Intermediate = Intermediate; Advanced = Advanced), capped at the exercise's top level. That gives:
  - **La-Ga:** five-note in the Intermediate warm-up, big arpeggio in the Advanced one.
  - **Bratty Nay:** octave in the Intermediate warm-up, as today.
  - **Straw:** big arpeggio in the Advanced warm-up, 1-2-3-2-1 in the Cool-down (see section 5).

### 1.4 "Climbing a semitone per round" is not always what Train does

Train's ladder already climbs a half step per round, from one note above the bottom of your range to where the pattern still fits. But it stops at 14 rounds: when your range allows more, it skips notes so that it still fits in 14.
- **Example:** a two-octave range on the new 1-2-3-2-1 pattern has 20 possible rounds, so Train would climb in whole steps over 10 rounds.

**Proposal:** for every exercise in this spec, always climb a half step and drop the 14-round cap. Sixteen to twenty rounds is normal. As a measure of length, 20 rounds of 1-2-3-2-1 at 120 beats a minute (four short notes, a held last note, then Train's usual two-beat rest) takes 80 seconds. Robert decides whether this applies to these exercises only or to all of Train.

- The lip trills', which comes down by a different route.

The spec says "Train's existing octave-and-a-half arpeggio". The exercise with that name is Big climb ("Octave-and-a-half arpeggio / Big climb"), so the plan uses Big climb's. Robert to confirm.

## 5. Things in the spec I think are wrong for a singer

1. **The phone's height.** The tension check and posture steps say to keep the eyes and chin level, but a singer watching the pitch line on a phone in their hand or on a table looks down. Looking down drops the chin and pushes the neck forward, which is exactly the tension the check looks for. Suggest adding "Prop your phone up at eye level." to both (in "You'll need" or as a first step). I think this is the most important point here.
2. **Rolling the head (B11, step 4).** Full circles that tip the head back press on the back of the neck. The usual safe version is half circles in front only. Suggested wording: "let your head drop forward and roll loosely side to side." The level is also scored, and a rolling head can't watch the line, so expect a lower score on that part.
3. **The wall check (B8).** Some bodies can't get the back of the head to the wall without tipping the chin up, which the same exercise and the tension check warn against. Suggest adding: "If your head only reaches the wall by lifting your chin, stop just short."
4. **"Overdo it" (A2).** "Too bratty works better than halfway" can turn into louder and pushed. Suggest adding three words: "Overdo the brat, not the volume." Today's "Making it heavy" warning only partly covers this.
5. **Straw in the Cool-down.** The ladder only climbs. Once Straw is a sung exercise, the Cool-down would climb toward the top of the range, which is the opposite of cooling down. Proposal: Straw at Beginner (1-2-3-2-1) in the Cool-down. Robert may prefer to take it out of the Cool-down.
6. **Scoring the tension check (B7).** Its purpose is to notice tension, not to hit notes. A score pulls attention to accuracy and invites pushing on the high rounds, which the steps warn against. Suggest the same "line, no score" as Chew and hum.

**Robert's 3 Nov rule.** In my reading none of the twelve items needs a demonstration. The two closest are Bratty Nay and the bright "a", where the singer has to know what "bright" sounds like. The whiny-child image covers it, and Save take lets them hear themselves back.

## 6. Train's current wording, word for word

From the app as on main (commit b864a06), for the review chat to check the merged text against. "Goal" and "How" are on the page; "Why", "Watch for" and "Care" are behind "More about this". Quotes are the app's exact text.

### A1. Slack-jaw La-Ga
- **How it runs today:** Agility, Beginner, Quiet; five-note scale at 132 a minute; "la" and "ga" one a note.
- **Title:** "Slack-jaw La-Ga"
- **Syllable:** "la"
- **Goal:** "Jaw hangs still. Only the tongue moves."
- **How:** "Hang your jaw still, and a knuckle under your chin will feel it."
- **Why:** "L uses the tip of your tongue and G uses the back of it. Alternating them quickly demands that the front and back of your tongue move independently — and if your jaw is doing the work, it physically cannot keep up. So hanging the jaw still forces the tongue to do its own job. That matters because your tongue is attached to your larynx, so every jaw-driven tongue movement is stealing from your vowel and pitch control."
- **Watch for:** "The jaw closing slightly on every consonant — universal at first, and exactly the habit being broken. Jutting the jaw forward instead of letting it drop back. Clenching it open: rigid is not the same as relaxed."
- **Care:** "If your jaw hinge clicks or aches, use a smaller opening. Never force it wide."
- **Syllables:** "la", "ga"

### A2. Bratty Nay
- **How it runs today:** Tone, Intermediate; octave arpeggio (1-3-5-8-5-3-1) at 120 a minute.
- **Title:** "Bratty Nay"
- **Syllable:** "nay"
- **Goal:** "A bratty, nasal "nay". Ugly on purpose."
- **How:** "Make it bright and nasal, never heavy, as you pass where your voice flips."
- **Why:** "Two things at once. The N gets your folds vibrating easily and forward. The brat recruits twang — a narrowing right at the top of your larynx that bunches the sound energy into the 2.5–5 kHz band, exactly where the ear is most sensitive. The result is that your voice gets much louder to a listener without any more air pressure or effort. That is why it gets you across the break and into a mix without shouting."
- **Watch for:** "Making it heavy instead of bright — carrying chest weight up rather than letting the twang do the work. Forcing the nasal quality instead of letting it happen."
- **Care:** "This is supposed to sound ugly. Sharp, piercing, whiny, annoying. It is not a finished sound and you should not sing songs like this — it is a temporary tool to find a coordination, and you drop it once you have found it."

### A3. Straw singing
- **How it runs today:** Warm-up, Beginner, Quiet; a 3-minute guided clock, not scored.
- **Title:** "Straw phonation"
- **Plain name:** "Straw singing"
- **Syllable:** "oo"
- **You'll need:** "You'll need a straw."
- **Goal:** "Sing through a thin straw."
- **How:** "Seal your lips round the straw and let no air escape."
- **Why:** "The same idea as lip trills, but the straw sets the resistance for you. It is the best-researched exercise in the app."
- **Watch for:** "Air escaping around the straw, air escaping through the nose, and making the accents in your throat instead of from your belly."
- **Care:** "A narrow straw into water is a heavy load — build up to it. If you have a diagnosed voice problem, do this with a professional, not an app."

### A4. Tongue-out singing
- **How it runs today:** Tone, Beginner, Quiet; five notes down at 110 a minute.
- **Title:** "Tongue-out singing"
- **Syllable:** "uh"
- **Goal:** "Sing with your tongue out and down."
- **How:** "Stick your tongue out and down toward your chin, and keep it there."
- **Why:** "Your tongue is attached to a bone that is attached to your larynx. A bunched, retracted tongue root physically pulls on your larynx. Sticking your tongue out and down stops the root bunching backward, so for the first time you get to hear what your voice sounds like without that interference."
- **Watch for:** "The tongue creeping back in as you go up. That creep is the diagnosis. Only the tip out, so the root is still bunched. Trying for a beautiful tone — it will sound odd, and that is fine."
- **Care:** "Do not yank your tongue. It is a diagnostic posture, not a way to perform."

### A5. Mum
- **How it runs today:** Tone, Beginner, Quiet; five-note scale at 120 a minute.
- **Title:** "Mum"
- **Syllable:** "mum"
- **Goal:** "A hum that opens into a vowel."
- **How:** "Keep the hum buzzing in your lips as you open."
- **Why:** "The M is a hum, so your lips are closed and the sound has to start gently — you physically cannot slam into an M. That sets your folds going in a low-pressure state and then hands that setup over to the vowel. The M at the end is the clever part: it lets you check the resonance is still there after the vowel, rather than using the M just to launch."
- **Watch for:** "The vowel drifting toward "mam" or "mom" as you go up. That drift is a sign the outer neck and jaw muscles have started gripping. Also pressing the lips hard, which chokes it."
- **Care:** "Very safe. The best first exercise in most sessions."

### A6. Messa di voce
- **How it runs today:** Breath, Advanced; five held notes of 10 seconds spread through your range, scored on pitch.
- **Title:** "Messa di voce"
- **Plain name:** "Soft, loud, soft"
- **Syllable:** "ah"
- **Goal:** "One note: quiet, to full, back to quiet."
- **How:** "Swell from soft to full and back while only the volume changes."
- **Why:** "Getting louder and softer without the note changing is hard, because the natural coupling is that more pressure means higher pitch and a different quality. Learning to uncouple them is the best single test that your breath and your larynx are cooperating rather than compensating for each other. Only the volume should change — pitch, colour and vibrato all stay put."
- **Watch for:** "Going sharp on the way up and flat on the way down. The vibrato speeding up or vanishing. A lopsided shape — the quiet half on the way back down is the hard half. Breaking into a different register at the quiet end."
- **Care:** "Do not take the loud part to maximum. This is about control at the edges, not peak volume."
